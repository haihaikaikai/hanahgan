Section 01

Problem Description & Significance

A regional food bank operates a central warehouse and must decide each month how many pounds of food to send to each of its partner agencies — pantries, soup kitchens, and shelters — spread across a multi-county service area. Each month the warehouse receives a fixed supply of donated food. Each partner agency serves a measurable population of food-insecure residents and has a physical ceiling on how much food it can receive and store. Transporting food costs money, and the food bank operates under a hard transportation budget.

The central question: How many pounds of food should be allocated to each agency each month to maximize total food distributed to people in need — subject to warehouse supply, agency intake capacity, a transportation budget, and an equity floor guaranteeing every agency a proportional minimum?

🏠
Scale of Need
Across the 10 Kansas counties modeled here, 167,820 people experienced food insecurity in 2023 — real numbers from Feeding America's Map the Meal Gap study. That's roughly the entire population of Lawrence, KS going hungry.
📦
The Allocation Problem is Real
Without a systematic approach, food piles up at cheap-to-reach urban agencies while rural counties go underserved. Transportation costs and supply constraints create genuine tradeoffs that benefit from formal optimization.
🎯
Who Benefits
Food bank operations managers, nonprofit directors, and municipal grant officers. A solved LP can be rerun monthly as supply fluctuates — a practical operational tool, not just an academic exercise.
⚖️
Equity as a Constraint
Pure cost minimization concentrates food at easy-to-reach locations. The model encodes a fairness floor — every agency's food-insecure population receives at minimum a proportional baseline — making equity a hard constraint, not an afterthought.

Inspiration

Harvesters — The Community Food Network is the regional food bank serving northeastern Kansas and northwestern Missouri across 27 counties. Their publicly audited financials, Feeding America's county-level Map the Meal Gap 2025 dataset, and ATRI trucking cost benchmarks together provide a rare combination of real, citable parameters at every level of the model. This is one of the few humanitarian operations problems where genuine data exists at every level rather than having to be fabricated wholesale. The problem also maps directly onto the transportation LP framework covered throughout the semester.

Section 02

Model Development

The final LP formulation was reached through several iterations, each driven by a real tension in the data. The preliminary work explored stochastic approaches before constraints revealed that a deterministic LP was both more tractable and more appropriate for the operational problem.

— Preliminary exploration (stochastic) —
P1
Newsvendor Model — Statewide (all 105 Kansas counties)
The first approach adapted a classical Newsvendor model across all 105 Kansas counties. Using 2019–2023 Feeding America data, a linear trend regression was fit per county to project 2024 demand (μ); temporal residuals provided σ. With underage cost u = $2,500/person and overage cost o = $3.346/meal (derived from the dataset's Cost Per Meal column), the critical ratio CR ≈ 0.99866 drove allocations to the 99.87th percentile of each county's demand. The model correctly captured demand uncertainty but was operationally abstract: 105 independent county-level problems with no transportation cost structure.
P2
Infeasibility Discovery & MIP Reformulation
Adding a 2 lb/person/month floor across all 105 counties required ~785,000 lbs — well above the 600,000 lb budget. The problem was infeasible as stated. This reframed the question from "how much does each person get" to "who gets served at all." A MIP was formulated with binary variables bi indicating full service, maximizing Σ bi·μi weighted by food insecurity rate. Result: ~19 of 105 counties fully served, covering ~300,000 persons. The MIP was mathematically correct but operationally thin — it lacked the transportation cost structure needed for real dispatch decisions.
— Final LP formulation (deterministic) —
1
Shift to Harvesters LP — Real Agency Data
Ingesting the Harvesters project proposal revealed real per-agency transportation costs (derived from ATRI benchmarks and road distances), agency intake capacity ceilings (USDA FNS benchmarks), and a verified $30,000 monthly budget from Harvesters' FY2024 audited financials. This enabled a cleaner LP scoped to 10 real partner agencies. Food is measured in pounds (highly divisible), so the LP relaxation is exact — no binary variables required.
2
Budget Concentration Problem
The first LP run produced a degenerate solution: 318,240 lbs (53% of supply) flowed to Wyandotte County at just $0.0013/lb, spending only $8,716 of the $30,000 budget. Every other agency received exactly its equity floor. The model was feasible and optimal by its own objective — but operationally meaningless. The $21,284 of unspent budget represented a missed equity opportunity, not savings.
3
Budget Floor Constraint — The Key Equity Insight
The transportation budget is a resource to be deployed for equity, not merely a ceiling to stay under. A budget floor constraint Bmin = $20,000 was added, requiring at least $20,000 to be spent on transportation. This forced the solver to route ~$11,284 of additional spend beyond its natural preference — compelling meaningful food volume to expensive rural agencies and producing a genuinely equitable distribution. The $20,000 floor was verified to sit above the natural cheapest-fill cost (~$8,716) and below the maximum feasible spend (~$25,965), ensuring genuine optimization tension without infeasibility.

Scalar Parameter Derivations

Supply S = 600,000 lbs/month. Harvesters distributed 60 million lbs in calendar year 2024 across its full 27-county service area. The 10 modeled Kansas counties account for 167,820 of ~374,900 regional food-insecure persons (44.8%). Scaling: 60M × 44.8% / 12 × 1.34 capacity factor = 600,000 lbs/month. Source: KCUR News, March 2025; Harvesters operational data.

Transportation cost cj. Formula: (road distance in miles × ATRI 2023 benchmark $2.27/mile) ÷ 3,500 lb average load. Source: ATRI Operational Costs of Trucking 2023; road distances via Google Maps.

Agency capacity Cj. Per-agency intake capacity is not publicly reported. Estimated as a multiple of dj by size tier: large urban (dj > 20,000) = 12×dj; mid-sized (5,000 < dj ≤ 20,000) = 15×dj; small rural (dj ≤ 5,000) = 10×dj. Consistent with USDA FNS benchmarks and Harvesters' network average of ~8,300 lbs/agency/month across ~600 programs.

Budget B = $30,000/month. From Harvesters' FY2024 audited Statement of Functional Expenses: vehicle cost and freight = $1,169,014/year, or $97,418/month for the full network. Scaled to 10 counties: $97,418 × 44.8% × 0.69 routing efficiency factor = $30,000/month.

Budget floor Bmin = $20,000/month. Policy constraint. Set above the natural cheapest-fill cost (~$8,716) and below the maximum feasible spend (~$25,965) to guarantee genuine optimization tension without infeasibility.

Equity floor r = 2 lbs/person/month. At 1.2 lbs/meal (USDA standard), this represents approximately 1.7 meals/month per food-insecure resident. Leaves the optimizer significant room above the floor while ensuring no county receives zero allocation.

PuLP Implementation

The model is implemented in Python using PuLP, following the STEP 1 / STEP 2 structure used throughout the course. The budget floor is computed programmatically before the problem is defined, ensuring the parameter is transparent and reproducible.

STEP 1 — Problem Definition
# Install: !pip install pulp --quiet from pulp import * prob = LpProblem("Harvesters_Food_Allocation", LpMaximize) # Agency data (sources: Feeding America 2025; ATRI 2023; Harvesters FY2024) agencies = ["Wyandotte", "Johnson", "Shawnee", "Douglas", "Riley", "Leavenworth", "Miami", "Franklin", "Pottawatomie", "Atchison"] d = [26940, 64070, 25300, 16680, 11710, 10400, 4000, 3520, 2840, 2360] c = [0.0013, 0.0149, 0.0422, 0.0285, 0.0811, 0.0182, 0.0272, 0.0389, 0.0649, 0.0357] # Capacity tiers: large urban 12x; mid-sized 15x; small rural 10x C = [12*p if p>20000 else 15*p if p>5000 else 10*p for p in d] S = 600000 # supply ceiling (lbs/month) B = 30000 # budget ceiling ($/month) B_min = 20000 # budget floor — policy equity constraint r = 2 # equity floor (lbs/person/month) n = len(agencies) # Decision variables x = [LpVariable(f"x_{agencies[j]}", lowBound=0, cat='Continuous') for j in range(n)] # Objective: maximize total pounds distributed prob += lpSum(x[j] for j in range(n)), "Total_Pounds_Distributed" # Constraint 1: Supply prob += lpSum(x[j] for j in range(n)) <= S, "Supply" # Constraint 2: Agency capacity for j in range(n): prob += x[j] <= C[j], f"Capacity_{agencies[j]}" # Constraint 3: Budget ceiling prob += lpSum(c[j]*x[j] for j in range(n)) <= B, "Budget_Ceiling" # Constraint 4: Budget floor (equity deployment) prob += lpSum(c[j]*x[j] for j in range(n)) >= B_min, "Budget_Floor" # Constraint 5: Equity floor — 2 lbs/person minimum at every agency for j in range(n): prob += x[j] >= r*d[j], f"EquityFloor_{agencies[j]}"
STEP 2 — Solve and Print Results

Section 07

Solution Analysis

What the Budget Floor Actually Bought

The most direct way to understand the budget floor's effect is to compare the unconstrained solution (Bmin = $0) against the final solution (Bmin = $20,000). Without the floor, the optimizer poured 318,240 lbs — 53% of total supply — into Wyandotte alone at $0.0013/lb, spending only $8,716 in transport. The floor redirected all 264,360 of those above-floor pounds out of Wyandotte and into three rural agencies:

Every pound that left Wyandotte went to a county with a higher food insecurity rate — Douglas at 14.0%, Riley at 16.3%, and Pottawatomie at 11.0% — and each of those counties is between 44 and 125 miles from the warehouse. The $11,284 in additional transport spend is the literal dollar cost of equity in this model.

The disparity — Douglas at 12.76 lbs/person versus Johnson at 2.00 lbs/person — is an artifact of the optimizer finding the cheapest path to spend the budget floor. This per-person imbalance is a known limitation of the current formulation: a proportional cap constraint (xj ≤ α · (dj/Σdj) · S) could spread the above-floor allocation more evenly if equity across persons rather than across agencies is the priority.

Transport Budget Concentration

Despite serving only 2 of 10 agencies, Douglas and Riley together consume 65% of the entire $20,000 transport budget. The remaining 8 agencies share the other 35%. This concentration is not a flaw — it is the budget floor working as intended, routing dollars toward the agencies that cost the most to serve. Section 08

Key Insights & Interpretation

The optimizer chose Douglas over Riley for the bulk of above-floor allocation. Douglas County (Lawrence, $0.0285/lb) is cheaper per pound than Riley (Manhattan, $0.0811/lb), so the solver can spend the same budget dollars moving more total pounds through Lawrence. This is a genuine optimization result: rural equity doesn't mean sending food to the most remote location — it means deploying the budget to reach the most people per dollar above the floor.

The $30,000 budget ceiling is not actually reachable given the supply cap. Even routing all food in descending cost order, the maximum achievable transport spend is ~$25,965 — because 600,000 lbs of supply runs out first. Setting Bmin = $20,000 creates genuine tension without risking infeasibility: it forces $11,284 of additional spend beyond the natural cheapest-fill solution while remaining safely below the feasible maximum.

The equity floor and the budget floor work together and are both necessary. The equity floor (r = 2 lbs/person) guarantees every agency a baseline. The budget floor ensures meaningful food reaches expensive rural counties above that baseline. Without both, the LP degenerates into a trivial solution: pour everything into Wyandotte at $0.0013/lb and stop.

Operational Utility

The solved LP can be rerun monthly as warehouse supply fluctuates. Harvesters operations staff adjust S and resolve — the constraint structure immediately reveals which constraint is the binding bottleneck in any given month. A supply shadow price of 1.0 (as observed here) means every additional pound donated to the warehouse translates directly to one additional pound reaching food-insecure residents, with no leakage. This makes the model a practical argument for donor solicitation as much as a dispatch tool.

Limitations

Agency capacity ceilings (Cj) are estimated from USDA benchmarks rather than directly reported by Harvesters. The model assumes a single monthly allocation decision rather than dynamic restocking. The equity floor r = 2 lbs/person/month represents approximately 1.7 meals per food-insecure resident — a meaningful but modest baseline. Finally, the model is deterministic: it does not account for uncertainty in supply, which the preliminary Newsvendor formulation was designed to address but which was ultimately out of scope for the 10-agency operational problem.
